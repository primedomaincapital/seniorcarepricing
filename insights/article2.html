Paying for Senior Care: The Options Families Don't Know About

Every week, in every American city, the same scene plays out in a social worker's office: adult children, exhausted and frightened, learning for the first time that Medicare doesn't cover assisted living. That the $70,000-a-year facility their mother needs isn't covered by the health insurance she's paid into for forty years.

4 min read · Prime Domain Capital

The gap between what families assume and what's actually available is enormous. And buried in that gap are funding sources most families never discover until someone — a social worker, an elder-law attorney, a friend who's been through it — tells them.

What Medicare Doesn't Do (and What People Think It Does)

Let's be precise, because this is the most expensive misunderstanding in eldercare: Medicare does not cover assisted living, memory care, or long-term custodial care. It covers skilled nursing for up to 100 days after a qualifying hospital stay, with significant copays after day 20. That's it.

Roughly 60% of Americans over 65 believe Medicare covers long-term care. It doesn't. This single misconception derails more family financial plans than any other.

The Funding Sources That Actually Exist

1. Veterans Benefits (Aid & Attendance)

The most underutilized benefit in American eldercare. The VA's Aid & Attendance pension provides up to $2,300/month (veteran with spouse, 2026 rates) for wartime veterans who need assistance with daily living. It can be used for assisted living, memory care, or in-home care.

Eligibility: 90 days of active duty with at least one day during wartime, honorable discharge, medical need, and financial qualification. An estimated 1.5 million veterans qualify. Fewer than 10% apply. The application takes 6–9 months to process, so start early.

2. Medicaid HCBS Waivers

Medicaid does cover long-term care — but traditionally only in nursing homes. Home and Community-Based Services (HCBS) waivers let states use Medicaid dollars for assisted living and in-home care instead. Every state has them; waitlists range from months to years.

The catch: Medicaid has strict asset limits (typically $2,000 in countable assets for the applicant). Families with means need elder-law planning years in advance — the five-year lookback penalizes last-minute asset transfers.

3. Long-Term Care Insurance

The product everyone wishes they'd bought at 55. A good LTC policy purchased in your 50s costs $2,000–$4,000/year and covers $150,000–$300,000 in benefits. Purchased at 70, the same coverage costs triple — if you're still insurable.

Only about 7 million Americans hold LTC insurance. The market contracted sharply after insurers mispriced early policies, but the remaining carriers (Mutual of Omaha, Northwestern Mutual, New York Life) offer solid products. Hybrid life/LTC policies — life insurance with a long-term care rider — have become the more popular structure.

4. Home Equity

For homeowners, the house is often the largest care-funding asset. Options include:

  • Selling: The straightforward path. Median home sale proceeds can fund 3–5 years of assisted living in most markets.
  • HELOC or reverse mortgage: A reverse mortgage (HECM) lets homeowners 62+ tap equity without monthly payments. Controversial, sometimes mis-sold, but legitimate for the right situation — typically when the homeowner wants to age in place with home care rather than move.
  • Renting the home: If the parent moves to assisted living, renting their house can generate $1,500–$3,000/month toward care costs.

5. Family Cost-Sharing

Increasingly common and rarely discussed openly: siblings splitting the bill. The average family contribution to a parent's care exceeds $7,000/year in out-of-pocket costs plus enormous time commitments. Formalizing the arrangement — who pays what, who provides hands-on care, how decisions get made — prevents the resentments that destroy families during eldercare.

The Planning Window

"Long-term care planning has a cruel timeline: the time when you can best afford to plan is the time when it feels least urgent." Every funding option above works better — or only works — with advance planning. Aid & Attendance takes months to process. Medicaid has a five-year lookback. LTC insurance must be bought while you're healthy. Home equity strategies need time to execute.

The families who navigate senior care costs successfully share one trait: they started before the crisis. The conversation is uncomfortable. The alternative — making $8,000-a-month decisions from a hospital waiting room — is worse.
