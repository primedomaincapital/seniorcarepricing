Independent Living vs. Assisted Living: Where the Price Gap Really Opens

The two buildings sit on the same street, share the same dining room, and charge what looks like the same rent. One is independent living. The other is assisted living. The difference on the rate sheet is about $3,000 a month. The difference on the first invoice is closer to $4,500 — and by the third year, it can be $6,000. The gap between independent and assisted living is not a number. It is a slope, and it opens where families are least prepared to see it.

November 2026 · 7 min read · Prime Domain Capital

CareScout's 2025 Cost of Care Survey, released in March 2026 and built on more than 25,000 rates collected between July and November 2025, puts the national median for assisted living at $6,200 a month — $74,400 a year — up 5% on the year. Independent living, by contrast, runs about $3,200 a month nationally according to A Place for Mom's 2026 data, and about $3,000 on Genworth's 2025 figures. The headline gap is roughly $3,000 a month, or about double. That is the number families budget for. It is not the number families pay, because the gap does not open at move-in. It opens at the care assessment, and it widens every year after that.

What the base rate actually buys

Independent living is, at its core, a housing product with services attached: an apartment, meals, housekeeping, activities, transportation, and a social calendar. The resident is expected to manage their own medications, their own bathing, their own mobility. Assisted living is a care product with housing attached: the same apartment and meals, plus help with the activities of daily living — bathing, dressing, toileting, transferring, eating — delivered by staff around the clock. The $3,000 gap is the price of the care, and it is the only part of the bill that is fixed at move-in.

What is not fixed is everything the care assessment adds. Most assisted living communities price care in levels — typically three to five — and the level is set by an assessment that happens at move-in and is repeated, usually annually, or after any change in condition. A resident who arrives needing help with one activity and, six months later, needs help with three has moved up a level, and the monthly rate has moved with them. The assessment is clinical, not commercial, and the family does not negotiate it. The gap between independent and assisted living is therefore not a single number but a range, and the range is set by the resident's health, which is the one variable the family cannot control.

The add-ons compound the gap. Medication management — the staff ordering, storing, and administering prescriptions — is typically an additional charge, and it is charged per medication or per administration, so a resident on eight prescriptions pays more than a resident on two. Incontinence care, two-person transfers, and overnight checks are each priced separately in many communities. A resident who needs all three can see the monthly bill rise by $1,000 to $1,500 above the quoted base rate before the first month is over. The family that budgeted the $6,200 median is paying $7,500, and the gap to the $3,200 independent living rate is no longer $3,000. It is $4,300.

Where the gap really opens

The gap opens widest at the transition from assisted living to memory care. A Place for Mom's 2026 data puts memory care at $6,690 a month against $5,419 for assisted living — but the CareScout figures tell a sharper story, because they are built on the same survey. CareScout's assisted living median of $6,200 implies a modeled memory care range of $7,440 to $8,060 a month at 20% to 30% above assisted living — a premium of $1,240 to $1,860 a month for the secured environment, the higher staffing ratios, and the specialized programming. Against the $3,200 independent living median, the memory care bill is roughly two and a half times the independent living rate. The family that moved a parent into independent living at $3,200 and, three years later, is paying $7,800 for memory care has seen the monthly cost rise by 144% — and the rise happened in steps, each one clinically justified, none of them budgeted.

The geography of the gap matters as much as its size. In the San Jose metro area, the assisted living median is $10,495 a month against a California statewide median of $7,000 — a $3,495 premium for one metro, before any care-level add-ons. A family in San Jose is paying, for assisted living, roughly what a family in Mississippi pays for memory care. The independent living rate follows the same map, so the gap between the two levels is roughly constant in percentage terms — but the absolute dollars are not, and the dollars are what the family writes the check for. The gap opens widest where the cost of living is highest, which is also where the families with the least room to absorb it tend to live.

The annual increase is the third force that widens the gap, and it widens it asymmetrically. Assisted living rates have risen 3% to 5% a year in recent years, with some operators reporting 5% to 8% — and the increases compound on a larger base. A 5% increase on a $6,200 assisted living rate is $310 a month; the same 5% on a $3,200 independent living rate is $160. After five years at 5%, the assisted living rate has risen by $1,711 a month and the independent living rate by $884. The gap has grown by $827 a month without any change in the resident's condition, and without any change in the care level. The gap widens even when nothing goes wrong.

The counterargument: the gap is the care, and the care is the point

The case for the gap is straightforward, and it deserves its weight. Independent living is a lifestyle choice. Assisted living is a medical necessity, delivered by staff who are awake at 3 a.m., trained in dementia care, and licensed to administer medication. The $3,000 difference is not a markup. It is the cost of the people who make the building safe for someone who cannot live alone. A family that compares the two rates as if they were the same product is making a category error, and the error runs in the direction of underestimating the care. The gap is the price of safety, and safety is not negotiable.

That argument is right about the care and wrong about the transparency. The problem is not that assisted living costs more. The problem is that the family cannot see, at move-in, what it will cost in year three — because the care level, the add-ons, and the annual increases are all variables the family does not control and the community does not guarantee. The independent living rate is a price. The assisted living rate is a starting point, and the distance between the starting point and the final bill is the gap that catches families by surprise. A community that publishes its care-level pricing, its add-on schedule, and its historical annual increases is giving the family the information it needs to plan. A community that quotes only the base rate is giving the family a number that will be wrong by the second year.

For a family, the practical conclusion is to budget for the third year, not the first: take the assisted living base rate, add the care-level premium the assessment is likely to assign, add the medication and incontinence charges if they apply, and then compound the total at 5% a year for three years. That number — not the rate on the brochure — is the cost of assisted living. For an operator, it is to publish the full schedule, because the family that discovers the gap on the first invoice is the family that leaves a review, and the review is permanent. And for anyone in the business of senior care pricing, the missing number is now obvious. Nobody publishes the all-in cost of assisted living by care level, by market, over time. The firm that does will own the next argument in senior care.
