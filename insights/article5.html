90.4%: Senior Housing Just Crossed a Line It Hasn’t Touched Since 2014

An operator in Dallas walked a building in October 2026 that was finally full — not just full, but full with a waiting list, in a sector that spent the pandemic begging families to visit. Senior housing occupancy had just crossed 90% for the first time since 2014. The question in the room was not how to fill the beds. It was what to charge for them, and who would be able to pay.

November 2026 · 5 min read · Prime Domain Capital

Senior housing occupancy reached 90.4% in the third quarter of 2026 across the 31 NIC MAP Primary Markets, the National Investment Center for Seniors Housing & Care reported on October 1. It was the first time primary-market occupancy had been above 90% since 2014, and the 21st consecutive quarter of gains. Independent living reached 91.7%. Assisted living, the segment that lagged longest, rose twice as fast as independent living in the quarter, up 0.8 percentage points to 89.1%. Occupied units increased to 644,428. Four primary markets, San Francisco, Chicago, Kansas City, and Dallas, set record highs.

The milestone matters less as a round number than as a verdict on a decade of argument. After the pandemic emptied buildings and bankrupted operators, the industry spent five years debating whether demand would return or whether families had permanently lost trust in congregate care. The demand returned. The supply did not.

The supply drought behind the milestone

Inventory growth remained near record lows in the third quarter, below 1.0% year over year for the sixth consecutive quarter. In the second quarter, year-over-year inventory growth was only 0.4%, with fewer than 16,000 units under construction. New construction starts fell 1% in the third quarter, to 16,159 units, split between independent living and assisted living. NIC MAP tracked only 2,200 new active adult units opening in the first three quarters of 2026 across 880 communities.

Against that drought, demand has been relentless. Annual net absorption has averaged 32,000 units since 2022, about 50% above the pre-2020 record of under 22,000 units, and every quarter since 2022 has topped the strongest pre-pandemic period on record. The first baby boomers turned 80 in 2026, kicking off what NIC MAP calls the steepest stretch of 80-plus population growth in the nation's history. Occupancy has risen more than 200 basis points in each of the last four consecutive years, a streak no other major commercial property type has matched over the same period.

The pricing consequence is already visible. Senior housing rents have grown 28.8% cumulatively since pre-pandemic baselines, according to JLL's 2026 survey, outpacing most other commercial real estate sectors. In-place rent growth in the second quarter of 2026 ran 3.8% year over year for independent living and 5.3% for assisted living, and annual rent growth dipped only slightly in the third quarter, to 4.6% from 4.7%. CBRE's H2 2025 investor survey found that rental rates making new development feasible remain approximately 15% to 20% above current market rents in most core markets. The market is telling developers the rents are not yet high enough to build, and telling families the rents are already high enough to hurt.

The money follows the occupancy

Investors have noticed. Welltower, the largest senior housing REIT, reported second-quarter 2026 normalized funds from operations up 25% year over year to $1.60 per diluted share, with total same-store net operating income up 15.5% and its Seniors Housing Operating portfolio up 20.5%, on 9.2% organic revenue growth supported by 330 basis points of occupancy growth and 5.2% growth in revenue per occupied room. Healthcare REITs more broadly have ridden the same wave, with occupancy gains, limited construction, and growing demand for specialized care reshaping the sector.

But the operating picture is more divided than the occupancy headline suggests. NIC's September 2026 analysis found significant dispersion in property-level performance, particularly in assisted living, while labor remains the industry's largest operating expense. Median operating margins have largely recovered toward pre-pandemic benchmarks rather than breaking beyond them. In assisted living, the lower quartile of 2025 property-level margins remained negative while the upper quartile exceeded 40%. The same occupancy rate can describe a thriving building and a struggling one. The difference is staffing, pricing discipline, and care-level management, the unglamorous work that decides whether a full building is a profitable one.

The counterargument: full does not mean affordable

Here is the uncomfortable part. The national median for assisted living reached $5,900 per month in 2026, according to Genworth's Cost of Care Survey, $70,800 a year, $212,400 over three years. Rents up 28.8% since the pandemic, occupancy at a twelve-year high, and construction at a standstill add up to a market where the families who need care most will find fewer beds at higher prices. The industry solved its occupancy problem and created an affordability problem in the same motion.

Operators have a fair defense: the cost of delivering care has risen faster than the price of the room. Labor is the largest expense, and the staffing ratios that keep residents safe cannot be automated away. A community charging $8,200 a month may genuinely deliver more value than one charging $4,800. But that defense confuses the cost of care with the price of opacity. Families are not asking operators to charge less. They are asking to know what the second year costs, what the care-level escalation looks like, and what happens when a resident progresses from Level 1 to Level 3. Those are questions the industry can answer, and mostly does not.

The transparency picture is improving at the edges. State legislatures are considering standardized rate disclosures. Cost-comparison platforms are modeling total cost of care over time rather than month-one prices. The generation now entering assisted living, early baby boomers, comparison-shops healthcare the way it comparison-shops everything else. Operators who provide clarity are winning on occupancy. Those who do not are losing to those who do.

What changes now

Three implications follow from 90.4%. First, the question for families shifts from whether there is a bed to what the bed will cost in year three. A building that is 90% full does not negotiate. A family that budgets on today's rate and discovers a 5% annual escalation two years later has a problem no occupancy statistic can solve.

Second, the development math is the story to watch. Until rents rise another 15% to 20%, or construction costs fall, the supply drought continues, and the drought is what keeps occupancy high. The industry is, in effect, rationing its own growth. That is good for existing owners and bad for the families who will need a bed in 2028.

Third, pricing data becomes more valuable, not less, as the market tightens. When buildings were empty, the price was negotiable and the information gap was survivable. When buildings are full, the family that knows the true cost of care, including escalation, has the only leverage left: the ability to choose before choosing becomes impossible. The milestone is real. So is the bill that comes with it.
