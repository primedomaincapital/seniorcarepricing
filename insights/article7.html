How Long Do Families Wait for a Senior Care Placement?

The marketing director said the building was full, but there was a waitlist. She did not say where on the list the family would stand, or how long the wait might be — because, in most communities, there is no answer to give.

November 2026 · 8 min read · Prime Domain Capital

The call came on a Thursday, from the marketing director of the assisted living community the family had toured twice. The building was full, she said, but there was a waitlist, and the family was welcome to join it. She did not say where on the list they would stand, how long the wait might be, or what would happen if the room that opened was a studio when the family needed a one-bedroom. She did not say those things because, in most communities, there is no answer to give. The list is not a queue. It is a roster, and the family had just joined the back of it without knowing what the front looks like.

That conversation is becoming the normal one. Senior housing occupancy reached 90.4% in the third quarter of 2026 across the 31 NIC MAP Primary Markets, the National Investment Center for Seniors Housing & Care reported on October 1 — the first time primary-market occupancy had been above 90% since 2014, and the 21st consecutive quarter of gains. Independent living reached 91.7%. Assisted living rose twice as fast as independent living in the quarter, up 0.8 percentage points to 89.1%. Occupied units increased to 644,428. The industry spent five years after the pandemic debating whether demand would return. It returned; the supply did not, and the wait is where families now meet that fact.

How long the wait is depends on a question most families never think to ask: a wait for what, exactly? A bed in any building, a room in one specific community, a memory care suite, or a subsidized slot all have different clocks, and the national numbers describe only the first of those.

What 90.4% means on the ground

Occupancy is an average, and averages hide the map. Sixteen of the 31 primary markets NIC MAP tracks are now above 90% occupancy, up from 15 in the second quarter. Boston leads at 94%, followed by San Francisco at 93.3% and Minneapolis at 92.7%. At the other end, Houston sits at 87.3%, Atlanta at 87.2%, and Miami at 86.5%. A family searching in Boston is competing for the last one in ten rooms; in Miami, roughly one in seven is open.

The supply side explains why the map is tightening rather than loosening. Inventory growth stayed below 1.0% year over year for the sixth consecutive quarter, and in the second quarter it was only 0.4%, with fewer than 16,000 units under construction. New construction starts fell 1% in the third quarter, to 16,159 units. Against that drought, annual net absorption has averaged 32,000 units since 2022, about 50% above the pre-2020 record of under 22,000 units. The first baby boomers turned 80 in 2026, opening what NIC MAP calls the steepest stretch of 80-plus population growth in the nation's history. NIC MAP's market outlook puts the investment needed to meet demand at more than $1 trillion through 2050 — the buildings families will need in 2028 are mostly not under construction today.

McKnight's Senior Living, reporting the NIC MAP release, put the consequence in one sentence: some high-occupancy markets are turning to waitlists, and doing so leaves older adults waiting years for housing to become available. That is the honest national answer to how long placement takes. Not weeks. In the tightest markets, years.

The turnover paradox: full buildings still open rooms

Here is the fact that keeps the wait from being a dead end. A full building is not a sealed building. Senior Housing News reported in March 2025 that the median annual resident turnover rate in assisted living was approximately 46.8% in 2024, based on data from Direct Supply. At that rate, a 100-resident community loses roughly four residents a month — to moves, to higher levels of care, to death. The rooms open. The question is who is standing ready when they do.

That turnover is why the practical timeline for a flexible family is far shorter than the headline suggests. Advisors in Tampa Bay report that a family willing to accept a twenty-minute drive radius can usually find a placement within a few weeks, even in a tight market — because the constraint is rarely the building. It is the specific room: the cheapest apartment in the most popular building, the ground-floor unit near the nursing station, the secured memory care suite. A family told there is a six-month wait is often being told there is a six-month wait for the cheapest unit, not for the building.

Memory care is the exception that proves the rule. Secured dementia care is the most reliably tight category in most markets, with fewer suites, longer stays, and lower turnover than general assisted living. In Charlotte, assisted living runs $4,200 to $5,800 a month in 2026, with memory care at $5,400 to $7,200, and the tighter corridors run fuller and pricier. The national memory care median tells the same story from the price side: A Place for Mom's 2026 data puts memory care at $6,690 a month against $5,419 for assisted living, and CareScout's 2025 assisted living median of $6,200 implies a modeled memory care range of $7,440 to $8,060 a month at 20% to 30% above it. The rooms that cost the most are also the rooms that open least often.

What a waitlist actually is

Most families picture a numbered queue: fourth in line, then third, then second. In Greater Charlotte, advisors say, that picture is wrong. Communities overwhelmingly run what is better described as an interest list — a roster of families who have expressed intent, sometimes with money attached, from which the community selects when a room opens. Selection is driven by fit, not by order of arrival. Each room has a shape — a studio on a secured hall, a semi-private room, a ground-floor unit near the nursing station. When one opens, the community looks for the resident whose assessed care needs, payment source, and move-in timing match that specific room. A family that signed up in June can be passed over in September by a family that signed up last week, and nothing improper has happened.

The money attached to the list matters, and families should know which kind they are paying. A waitlist or reservation deposit is usually a few hundred dollars, often described as refundable. A community fee — sometimes called a move-in or admission fee — is a larger charge collected at admission to cover assessment, apartment turnover, and onboarding, and it is frequently non-refundable in whole or in part once the resident moves in. A holding fee is rent paid to keep a specific room empty and off the market for a defined stretch of days, usually at or near the full daily room rate, while a parent finishes a rehab stay or a family sorts out a house sale. Three different payments, three different promises. The deposit buys consideration. Only the holding fee buys the room.

The practical advice follows from the mechanics. Working three or four lists at once is normal in a tight market, and the family that is ready — financial documents in hand, care assessment done, decision-makers aligned — is the family that gets the call when a room opens on a Tuesday and must be filled by Friday. Readiness, not seniority, is what the list rewards.

The real queues: where waiting is measured in years

Florida's Statewide Medicaid Managed Care Long-Term Care program is capped, and access runs through a screening performed by an Aging and Disability Resource Center. The waitlist is not first come, first served. The screening produces a priority score reflecting frailty and level of need, and the Department of Elder Affairs releases people from the list each month based on that score against the enrollment slots available — not based on how long they have been waiting.

California's Medi-Cal Assisted Living Waiver tells the same story. The program has historically carried a statewide waitlist, and although the state has added thousands of slots, some families still wait months or longer for an opening. Affordable senior housing — HUD 202, low-income housing tax credit properties, nonprofit buildings — carries waitlists that can span months to several years, depending on demand and income tier. These are the waits that no amount of flexibility shortens, because the constraint is funding, not fit.

That distinction is the honest answer to the question this article started with. There is no published national average wait time for assisted living placement — Florida's Agency for Health Care Administration, for example, licenses assisted living facilities but does not publish occupancy or waitlist data, and neither do individual operators. What exists instead is a range with a shape: weeks for a flexible private-pay family in a mid-occupancy market, months for a specific room in a popular building, three to twelve months for the most highly rated communities according to A Place for Mom's Cost of Waiting report, and years for subsidized slots.

The counterargument: the wait is shorter than the panic suggests

The case against the alarm deserves a fair hearing, because the numbers cut both ways. Occupancy at 90.4% still means nearly one room in ten is empty. Turnover at 46.8% a year means those vacancies refresh constantly. Rents are rising — 4.6% year over year in the third quarter, and 28.8% cumulatively since pre-pandemic baselines according to JLL's 2026 survey — but they are rising more slowly than they were, down from 4.7% in the second quarter.

There is also a genuine argument that the waitlist itself is partly theater. A community with a list looks desirable, and desirability fills rooms. The list that matters is the short one: the three or four communities the family has toured, where the staff know the parent's name and the paperwork is already done.

The counterargument is right about the mechanics and wrong about the direction. Turnover opens rooms, but absorption fills them faster — 32,000 units a year against 16,159 construction starts — and every quarter of that arithmetic makes the next search harder. The wait is short today for the family that is flexible, ready, and early. It is the family that starts after the fall, after the hospital discharge, after the diagnosis, that discovers what 90.4% actually costs: not money, but the loss of the choice itself.

What to do with the wait

Three implications follow. First, start 12 to 18 months before you think you need to. A Place for Mom's Cost of Waiting report documents the pattern: families who delay planning by a year to a year and a half typically pay more, not less — because a fall, a stroke, or a hospitalization forces immediate placement and removes the ability to compare, and a year of delay means starting at next year's rates. The search is cheapest when it is hypothetical.

Second, treat the wait as a project with a bridge, not as a period of doing nothing. Home care at the national median of $35 an hour buys time at $1,517 a month for 10 hours a week — and the average in-home arrangement lasts 15 to 20 months, according to Todd Austin of Activated Insights, roughly the length of the wait it is bridging. Respite stays, short-term rehabilitation beds, and shared housing can cover the gap between the decision and the room. The families who suffer in a wait are the ones who treat it as a pause. It is not a pause. It is the plan.

Third, widen the radius before widening the budget. The single most effective way to shorten a placement search is to accept a twenty-minute drive instead of a ten-minute one, and a second-choice floor plan instead of the cheapest studio. The room that opens is rarely the room the family pictured. The families who move in are the ones who decided, before the call came, which compromises they could live with — and who had the paperwork ready when the phone rang on a Thursday.
